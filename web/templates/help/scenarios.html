A scenario is a complete, named copy of the plan. Your workspace can hold as many as you like, and exactly one is active at a time — the one the editor, the dashboard and the reports show. Scenarios are how you answer "what if?" without destroying the plan you already believe.

The one-change method

{% call steps() %}
  • Get the base plan right first. Every scenario you copy from it inherits its mistakes.
  • Copy it. On the scenarios page, press the copy button on its row, or create a new scenario with Copy the active plan. The copy becomes active straight away.
  • Change one thing. Retire two years later, spend 10% less, switch the withdrawal policy. One change per scenario, or you will not know which change moved the answer.
  • Name it for the change — "Retire at 62", not "Plan 3".
  • Compare. Open the comparison and press Simulate all.

    {% call note('warn', 'Simulation results are not saved') %}

    Plans are stored in the database; simulation results are kept in the server's memory only, per scenario. They disappear when you edit that scenario (stale odds beside fresh inputs would mislead) and when RetPlan restarts. The "Success" column on the scenarios page says not run until you simulate again.

    The comparison page

    Every scenario in the workspace, one column each, with these rows:

    • Chance of success, with its ± margin (1.96 standard errors, a 95% interval), and a best tag on the highest. Blank until simulated.
    • Median final wealth and bad-luck final wealth (P5) from the simulation, in today's money.
    • From the deterministic projection, always current: the verdict at fixed returns, the age the money lasts to, the funded ratio, lifetime tax and final wealth at fixed returns.
    • Inputs worth checking at a glance: retirement age, savings today, first-year spending, and the withdrawal policy.

    Below the table, a chart of net worth at fixed returns for every scenario, and — once all of them have been simulated — a chart of the median simulated net worth.

    Why "Simulate all" exists

    Simulate all runs every scenario with 1,500 trials and the same random seed (20260916). That means every scenario faces exactly the same sequence of simulated markets: the same crash in the same year of trial 417, the same bull run in trial 1,002. The differences between columns are then caused by the plans, not by the dice.

    If you simulate scenarios one at a time from their dashboards instead, each run can use a different trial count or seed, and two nearly identical plans can differ by a few points purely from sampling noise. With a common seed that noise largely cancels in the comparison, which is why a 2-point difference between two columns is meaningful here even though each figure on its own carries a margin of about ±2 points.

    {% call note('trap', 'Do not compare a 200-trial run with a 1,500-trial run') %}

    Results from the dashboard stay attached to each scenario until it is edited, so the comparison can show a mix of runs. After editing any scenario, press Simulate all again so every column comes from the same run.

    {% endcall %}

    Reading the differences

    • Success moves, median wealth barely moves: the change mostly affects the bad paths — typical of retiring later, holding a cash buffer or a flexible withdrawal policy.
    • Median wealth moves, success barely moves: the change mostly affects the good paths — typical of a higher equity share.
    • The fixed-return verdict and the simulated odds disagree: the plan works on average but not in bad sequences. Believe the odds.

    See running a simulation for what the margins mean, and recipes for worked questions.
