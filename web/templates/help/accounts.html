Allocation and the glidepath

The lower table sets weights now and weights at the end of the glide, plus the ages the glide runs between. Weights are percentages of that account and should sum to 100 — the audit flags it if they do not.

Between the two ages the model interpolates linearly, so an account gliding from 80% equity at 55 to 45% at 70 is at about 62% at 62. Leave the end weights at zero for a fixed allocation.

{% call note('tip', 'Glide the household, not each account') %}

It is usually simpler to think about the whole portfolio's risk and then set each account's weights so the total lands where you want it. Holding bonds in one account and equities in another is fine — and often tax-efficient — provided you look at the aggregate.

Draw order: the decision that quietly costs the most

When spending exceeds income, the model works down the draw order, taking what it needs from each account in turn and grossing up for tax exactly. It respects:

  • forced minimum distributions, which are taken first whether you need them or not;
  • lock ages — a locked wrapper is skipped entirely;
  • early-withdrawal penalties, folded into the same solve;
  • the liquid flag — illiquid accounts are never sold.

Anything left unspent (a forced draw you did not need, or surplus income) is swept into the account nominated at the bottom of the page.

Illiquid assets

Property and businesses belong in a wrapper with Liquid unticked. They then appear in net worth and grow at their asset class's return, but are never sold to meet spending. If you intend to sell one, model it as a one-off income row in the sale year and reduce the account balance.

{% call note('trap', 'A plan that "fails" with money left') %}

If the audit says a shortfall occurred while liquid money remained, something blocked the drawdown: a lock age, an illiquid flag, or every account being empty except an illiquid one. That is usually a modelling error rather than a real funding failure.
