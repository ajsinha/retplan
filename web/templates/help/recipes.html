Each recipe is a short sequence that answers one real question. They assume your plan is entered and the audit passes.

Can I afford to retire at 60?

{% call steps() %}
  • On household, set the retirement age to 60.
  • On income, change the To age on each salary row to 60 as well. Nothing else stops the salary — this is the step people forget.
  • Check whether any pension has a penalty or lock age before 60 on wrappers. If it does, the bridge years have to come from somewhere else.
  • Run Full analysis on the dashboard and read the success probability and the earliest retirement age the solver found.
    • {% endcall %} {% call note('tip', 'Let the solver answer directly') %}

    Earliest retirement age does exactly this search for you: it bisects on retirement age, moving the salary end dates with it, until success hits your confidence level. Use it first, then test the specific age you care about.

    {% endcall %}

    How much can I actually spend?

    {% call steps() %}
  • Make sure the split between essential and discretionary rows is honest.
  • Run Full analysis. Read max sustainable spend.
  • Open the Success against spending chart. It prices each extra unit of spending in probability — usually the most useful picture in the app.
  • Decide where on that curve you want to sit. The solver aims at your confidence level; the curve shows what the next 5,000 a year would cost you.
    • {% endcall %}

    The figure is first-year spending in today's money, including debt service, and it scales every spending row proportionally. If you would rather cut only discretionary spending, change those rows directly and re-run.

    What if markets crash the year I retire?

    Two ways, and they answer different questions.

    The distribution already contains it

    With the crash process on, a share of the simulated futures already include a crash at or near retirement. The P5 and P10 bands on the fan chart are largely made of those paths — that is what the bottom of the fan is.

    Force it and look

    {% call steps() %}
  • On markets, raise the crash probability to 1 (a crash every year is not realistic, but it makes the mechanism visible) or deepen the depth distribution.
  • Re-run and compare the success probability and the depletion-age curve against your baseline.
  • Put the settings back. Write down the two numbers you compared.
    • {% endcall %} {% call note('warn', 'Change one thing at a time') %}

    Stress tests are only informative against a baseline you recorded. Download the plan JSON before you start experimenting so you can always return to it exactly.

    {% endcall %}

    Should I pay off the mortgage or invest?

    {% call steps() %}
  • Record the baseline: run a simulation and note the success probability and median terminal wealth.
  • Add an overpayment on debt equal to what you would otherwise invest, and reduce the matching contribution on accounts.
  • Re-run with the same seed and trial count, and compare.
    • {% endcall %}

    The comparison is fair only if the money comes from the same place in both runs. The usual finding: paying down debt is a guaranteed return equal to the loan rate, so it wins whenever the loan rate exceeds the portfolio's expected return after tax and fees — and it always wins on certainty, because it removes a fixed obligation from the bad paths.

    Is my adviser fee worth it?

    Set the adviser fee to zero on the markets page, re-run with the same seed, and compare median terminal wealth. The difference is what the fee costs you over the plan, in today's money. Whether it is worth it is not a question this model can answer — but the number it is being measured against should be this one, not the annual percentage.

    What does one more year of work buy?

    Push both retirement age and salary end age out by one year and re-run. One extra year typically moves the answer more than any plausible change in asset allocation, because it does three things at once: adds a year of saving, removes a year of drawdown, and shortens the horizon the money has to cover.

    How exposed am I to living a long time?

    Raise the planning age by five years on the household page and re-run. If the success probability falls sharply, longevity is your binding risk and the answer is usually guaranteed income (a later state pension claim, or an annuity) rather than a different portfolio.
