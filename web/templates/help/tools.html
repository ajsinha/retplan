These tools turn "is my plan all right?" into "what should I do about it?". Each changes a copy of your active plan and simulates it with the same random seed as the original, so any difference you see comes from the change, not from different luck. Nothing is saved unless you ask.

What if… (the dashboard sliders)

{{ goto('dashboard', 'Open the dashboard') }} The sliders move one thing at a time, or several together, and each move re-runs 800 futures in well under a second:

{{ field_table([ ("Retire", "Everyone retires this many years later or earlier.", "Salaries that stopped at retirement stop at the new age; spending rows that switched at retirement switch then too."), ("Spending", "Every spending row, up or down by this share.", "Essentials and wishes alike."), ("Save more each year until retiring", "Spend this much less each working year.", "Unspent income is already saved automatically, so saving more means spending less now; spending in retirement is unchanged."), ("Share of shares", "Moves this share of every account between shares and bonds.", "Glide paths move by the same amount."), ("Fees", "The platform fee, up or down.", "Fund costs are unchanged."), ("Start public pension", "Every public pension starts this many years later or earlier.", "The amount changes by 8% a year later and 6.67% a year earlier - the claiming explorer lets you set your country's rates."), ]) }}

The panel shows the chance of success and how it moved, the median and bad-luck final wealth, the age the money runs out in a typical failing future, and lifetime tax. Save as scenario keeps the adjusted plan as a new scenario and switches to it; the original is untouched, so you can {{ goto('compare', 'compare them') }}.

{% call note('why', 'Why 800 futures, and what "within noise" means') %}

800 futures answer in a fraction of a second, with an error bar of about ±1.5 points on the odds. Because every run uses the same seed, two runs of the same plan give exactly the same answer; but a real change smaller than about two error bars may be too small to trust, and is marked within noise. For the precise figure, save the scenario and run the full simulation.

Your biggest levers

On the dashboard, Find my levers tries each common change on its own - retire a year or two later or earlier, spend 5% or 10% less or 5% more, save more, 10% more or less in shares, lower fees, claim a public pension two years later or earlier - with 1,500 futures each, and ranks them by what they do to your odds. Rows that change the odds by less than the noise are faded. Click a row to load it into the sliders, combine it with others, and save the result as a scenario.

{% call note('tip', 'Read the list both ways') %}

The top of the list is where effort pays; the bottom is what to avoid. A lever with a small effect on the odds can still move median wealth a lot - fees usually do - which the sliders show.

When to claim a public pension

{{ goto('tool_claiming', 'Open the claiming explorer') }} Choose a public-pension income row (category state pension); its current start age and amount are taken as the usual ones. RetPlan then tries every age in the range you give, scaling the pension down by the early rate for each year before the usual age and up by the late rate for each year after, and runs your whole plan for each. The table gives the yearly amount, the chance of success, median and bad-luck final wealth, and the break-even age: how long you must live for the later, larger pension to have paid out as much in total.

  • US Social Security is roughly 6.7% less a year early and 8% more a year late; the UK State Pension cannot be taken early but grows about 5.8% a year deferred; other countries differ. Set the rates for yours.
  • Waiting usually wins with a long life expectancy and other money to live on meanwhile. Claiming early can win with thin savings or poor health.

Account conversions

{{ goto('tool_conversions', 'Open the conversion explorer') }} A conversion moves money from an account taxed on the way out (a traditional IRA, a 401(k), a pension) to one that is never taxed again (a Roth), paying the tax now. It pays when your tax rate now is lower than the rate you - or your heirs - would pay later, and it shrinks forced withdrawals later.

{% call steps() %}
  • Pick the from and to accounts and the ages to convert between - often the years between retiring and a pension starting, when income is low.
  • List some fixed amounts a year to try, and some taxable income levels to fill to - the tops of your lower tax bands. "Fill to 50,270" converts just enough each year to bring taxable income up to 50,270.
  • Set the tax your heirs pay on money left in accounts taxed on the way out. Comparing final wealth without it would favour never converting, because that money's tax would simply never be counted.
  • RetPlan runs your whole plan once per strategy and ranks them by the chance of success, then by after-tax final wealth. Try it saves a copy of your plan with that conversion as a new scenario.
    • {% endcall %}

    Conversions you keep appear in the editor under {{ goto('plan_section', 'Plan → Conversions', 'conversions') }}, where you can edit them. The tax on each is worked out with the rest of that year's income and paid by drawing on your accounts in the usual order.

    {% call note('warn', 'Rules this model does not know') %}

    RetPlan applies no country's conversion rules: waiting periods, income limits, conversion ladders and tax on benefits that rises with income are not modelled. Check the rules where you live before acting.

    {% endcall %}

    The suggested draw rate

    After every simulation the dashboard suggests how much of your savings to take in the first year you live on them - the year nobody in the household is paid for work any more. It is worked out for your plan, not borrowed from a rule of thumb:

    {% call steps() %}
  • RetPlan scales your spending in retirement from 40% to 200% of what you planned, leaving the working years (and so what you save) as they are, and runs your whole plan at each level: tax, pensions, forced withdrawals and all.
  • It finds the level at which the chance of success meets your confidence target, and the two levels at the guardrail odds either side - 10 points safer (cautious) and 15 points riskier (bold).
  • For each, the draw rate is that first year's withdrawal from savings - spending plus the tax on the withdrawal, less pensions and other income - divided by what your savings are worth at the start of that year, both in a typical future.
    • {% endcall %}

    The marker shows where your plan draws now. Try … as a scenario saves a copy of the plan with retirement spending at the suggested level. When pensions start a few years after you stop work, the first year's rate is higher than the years after; the card then gives the ten-year average too.

    {% call note('why', 'Why not just 4%?') %}

    The 4% rule came from US stocks and bonds over 30-year retirements, before tax and with no other income. Your answer depends on how long you plan for, your pensions, your tax and your mix, and the conservative market assumptions here - which is why it can be well under or over 4%. Once retired, use the spending check each year: it keeps the same odds as markets move.

    {% endcall %}

    Spending check: raise, hold or trim

    {{ goto('tool_spending', 'Open the spending check') }} Once a year, or after a big market move, ask how much you can spend. RetPlan runs your plan with all your spending scaled from 30% to 160% of today's, and draws three lines on the chance of success: your target (your plan's confidence level) and a lower and an upper guardrail around it - by default 15 points below and 10 above.

    • Inside the guardrails: carry on. Small market wobbles never become spending cuts.
    • Above the upper one: you can spend more - the page gives the amount that brings the odds back to the target.
    • Below the lower one: trim - by the amount that brings them back.

    Enter your savings today after markets have moved: every liquid account is scaled to that total before the test, so you need not update the plan first. This works best once you are retired; while you are still working, future pay and saving count for more than today's balance.

    Which account to draw from first

    {{ goto('tool_draw_order', 'Open the draw-order tool') }} The order in which you spend your accounts changes the tax you pay over a lifetime. RetPlan groups your accounts by how they are taxed, tries every order of those groups on the fixed-return path, simulates the best few (and your current order) in full, and compares them on the chance of success, lifetime tax and after-tax final wealth - money left in accounts taxed on the way out is reduced by the tax your heirs would pay. Try it saves a copy of your plan with that order; Use it changes the order in the plan itself (Plan → Accounts, "Draw order").

    {% call note('why', 'Why there is no single right answer') %}

    "Spend taxable money first, tax-free last" is a common rule, but low-income years before a pension starts, forced withdrawals later, and your own tax bands can all turn it around. That is why the tool tries every order on your plan rather than applying a rule.

    {% endcall %}

    Health costs and long-term care

    {{ goto('tool_health', 'Open the health and care tool') }} Three kinds of cost, tested before you add them:

    • Health cover until public cover starts - private insurance between retiring and, say, 65 in the US. An essential spending row.
    • Health costs in later life - premiums and out-of-pocket costs, rising faster than prices. An essential spending row with growth above inflation.
    • Long-term care - a chance, not a certainty. In each simulated future it happens or not, with your probability, starting at an age drawn between the two you give, and costs its full amount for its duration. That is how care really works, and why an average cost understates it: the risk is a few futures with very large bills. The fixed-return projection uses the expected cost.

    Test the effect shows the odds, wealth and tax with and without them; Add to my plan or Save as a scenario keeps them. Care risks are edited under {{ goto('plan_section', 'Plan → Health and care', 'care') }}.

    Net worth history

    {{ goto('networth', 'Open net worth history') }} A plan looks forward; this looks back.

    {% call steps() %}
  • Take a snapshot now and then - quarterly, or at each year-end. The form starts from your plan's accounts and loans and every portfolio's value; add a home, a car, other debts. A second snapshot on the same day replaces the first.
  • Watch it over time. With two or more snapshots, a chart shows assets, debts and net worth.
  • Portfolios record themselves. After every price collection each portfolio's value is stored, and kept for good - so its history grows beyond the year of daily prices RetPlan keeps.
  • Bring the plan up to date. To plan sets the plan's account and loan balances to a snapshot's, so the projection starts from where you really are.
