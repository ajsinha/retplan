Five ideas explain nearly every design decision in this app. None of them is difficult, and understanding them turns the output from a set of numbers into something you can argue with.

1. Real terms, path by path

All arithmetic happens in today's money. Where a simulation produces its own inflation path, results are deflated by that trial's realised inflation, not by an average — so a run where inflation spiked is penalised in the run where it spiked, rather than smeared across all of them.

The practical consequence: a "4% return" input and a "2.5% inflation" input mean your portfolio grows at about 1.5% in purchasing power. If that sounds low, the input is the problem, not the model.

2. A wrapper is when money is taxed

Instead of hard-coding account types, the model describes each as a wrapper with three decisions: is the contribution deducted, is growth taxed as it arises, and is the withdrawal taxable. Those three switches generate every common regime:

PatternInGrowthOutTypical name
EETExemptExemptTaxed Workplace pension, 401(k), RRSP
TEETaxedExemptExempt Roth, ISA, TFSA

This is why the app needs no country setting. See tax wrappers.

3. The essential floor

Every spending row is either essential or discretionary. The distinction is not cosmetic: flexible withdrawal policies are only allowed to cut the discretionary part. A plan whose spending is 100% essential cannot flex at all, and guardrails will do nothing for it.

{% call note('why', 'Why this matters more than the withdrawal rate') %}

Two households with identical portfolios and identical spending can have very different failure probabilities purely because one of them can cut 20% of its spending in a bad decade and the other cannot. Flexibility is a bigger lever than almost any investment decision, and it is free.

4. Sequence risk

The order returns arrive in matters enormously once you are withdrawing. Two paths with the same average return over thirty years produce wildly different outcomes depending on whether the bad years came first. Selling units to fund spending in a fallen market permanently removes the units that would have recovered.

This is why the app simulates whole paths rather than applying an average return, why the spaghetti chart exists, and why a crash the year you retire is a different event from the same crash ten years later.

5. Confidence, not certainty

A success probability is an estimate from a finite sample, and it comes with an error bar. The dashboard always shows it: "91.5% ± 1.2 points". Two consequences:

  • A difference of one or two points between two runs is noise, not a finding. Raise the trial count before concluding anything from a small gap.
  • 100% is not the target. A plan built to survive every simulated future is a plan that spends far too little in the overwhelming majority of them. The confidence level on the policy page is the dial for this trade.
{% call note('trap', 'Arithmetic mean is not what you earn') %}

Expected returns are entered as arithmetic means. What a portfolio actually compounds at is lower by roughly half the variance: 7% with 17% volatility compounds at about 5.6%. The dashboard reports both after a run, under "what you are actually assuming". If the compound figure looks wrong, your volatility input is doing something you did not intend.
