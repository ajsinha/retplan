A projection tells you the range of futures; a stress test tells you what one specific bad future would do. RetPlan replays five real crises, quarter by quarter, on the mix you hold today — the investable assets: every investment account's holdings, and the cash accounts as cash (property and debts are left out). It is the quickest way to find out whether you could live with your own portfolio in a bad year.

The five crises

CrisisQuartersWhat happened
Global financial crisis9 (2007 Q4 – 2009 Q4)Equities fell about 55% peak to trough; REITs worse; high-quality bonds rose.
Dot-com bust11 (2000 Q2 – 2002 Q4)A slow, grinding fall of about 45% over two and a half years, while bonds and property did well.
Covid crash2 (2020 Q1 – Q2)The fastest 30% fall on record, largely recovered the next quarter.
Rate shock4 (2022)Stocks and bonds fell together; the year a 60/40 portfolio had nowhere to hide.
Stagflation8 (1973 – 1974)Two years of falling stocks and double-digit inflation; commodities soared.

They are chosen to be different kinds of bad: a banking crash, a valuation bust, a sudden shock, an inflation shock that broke the bond hedge, and a long real-terms grind.

How your holdings are mapped

Each crisis is stored as quarterly total returns for five proxy indices:

  • equity: S&P 500
  • bond: Bloomberg US Aggregate
  • cash: 3-month US Treasury bills
  • property: FTSE Nareit All Equity REITs
  • commodity: S&P GSCI

Every holding follows the proxy for its asset class. Classes without a proxy of their own borrow one, scaled by a beta:

Asset classFollowsBeta
Equityequity1.00
Intl equityequity1.05
Emerging equityequity1.25
Cryptoequity2.50
Otherequity0.80
Bonds, Cash, Property, Commoditiestheir own proxy1.00

The beta scales the return in excess of cash, not the whole return:

rholding = rcash + β × (rproxy − rcash), floored at −95% a quarter

so a beta of 2 doubles the risk taken, not the interest earned on the cash part. A quarter where shares fell 20% and bills paid 1% becomes about −25% for emerging markets (1% + 1.25 × −21%).

The replay

The current mix, weighted by today's values, goes through the crisis one quarter at a time. It is rebalanced during the crisis according to the rebalancing setting of your last projection (annually by default). When the crisis ends, each holding grows at its expected return — the same one the projection would use, including any overrides — until the portfolio is back to today's value, or for at most 40 quarters (ten years).

There are no contributions, withdrawals or fees in a replay. That isolates the portfolio's own behaviour; the effect of spending through a crisis is what the Monte Carlo version below is for.

{% call note('warn', 'What the approximations leave out') %}
  • The figures are rounded index returns, good to about a point per quarter, and describe the shape and depth of each episode rather than a precise record. The 1973–74 figures are published annually and are spread evenly across each year's quarters, which smooths that crisis considerably.
  • Quarter-end values miss falls within a quarter. In March 2020 US shares were down about 34% at the low; the quarter ended down about 20%, and that is what you will see.
  • The proxies are US indices in dollars. A UK or European portfolio, and any currency effect, are approximated by the US experience.
  • All bonds follow investment-grade US bonds. High-yield or long-duration bonds behaved very differently in some of these crises.
  • Your individual holdings are replaced by their class. A single stock that went to zero in 2008 shows here as the index.
  • The crypto beta is a judgement: crypto did not exist in most of these episodes.
  • Recovery is to today's value in money, not in purchasing power. After the 1970s, getting the number back is not the same as getting the spending power back.
{% endcall %}

Opening every projection with a crisis

On the projection page, Open every trial with a crisis makes every simulated future start with the chosen crisis. For its first quarters every trial earns exactly the crisis returns; after that, returns are random as usual. Your contributions, withdrawals, fee and rebalancing all apply throughout, crisis included.

{% call steps() %}
  • Run a projection with your contributions and withdrawals, and no crisis. Name it "Base".
  • Run it again with a crisis chosen and the same seed. Name it after the crisis.
  • Compare the two in Saved runs: the median, the P10, and — if you are withdrawing — the chance of running out.
    • {% endcall %} {% call note('why', 'Why this is the honest test of sequence risk') %}

    The same crisis does far more damage at the start of a withdrawal plan than in the middle of it. Selling to fund spending while prices are down turns a temporary fall into a permanent one: the units sold are not there for the recovery. A portfolio with no withdrawals barely notices when its crash happens; a portfolio paying an income can be broken by the timing alone. Putting the crisis first, and then letting the future be uncertain, is exactly the case a retirement plan has to survive.

    {% endcall %}
