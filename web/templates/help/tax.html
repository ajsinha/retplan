Tax here is a table of bands. There is no country setting, no tax year, and no built-in rates — you type the schedule your jurisdiction applies.

Bands are in gross-income space

Each row is "from this income level, the marginal rate is this". The first band must start at 0. A tax-free allowance is therefore a 0% first band, not a separate field:

Income fromMarginal rateWhat it represents
00%the tax-free allowance
12,57020%basic rate
50,27040%higher rate
100,00060%the allowance taper, as its effective rate
125,14045%additional rate

An allowance taper — where the allowance is withdrawn above a threshold — is just a band carrying the higher effective marginal rate over the range where it bites. The example above is the UK's 60% band expressed exactly that way.

{% call note('why', 'Why bands, rather than an allowance field') %}

Because it keeps the schedule piecewise linear and strictly increasing, and that is what makes it exactly invertible. The model constantly needs to answer "how much must I withdraw so that, after tax, I can spend X?" With an invertible schedule that is a single calculation. With special-case allowance and taper logic it becomes a circular reference that a spreadsheet can only solve iteratively and non-deterministically. Expressing everything as bands buys exactness.

How tax is actually applied

Each year the model stacks your taxable income — employment, pensions, the taxable share of withdrawals — and applies the bands to the total. Two consequences worth internalising:

  • The marginal rate on a withdrawal depends on everything else you received that year. Taking 20,000 from a pension in a year with a large salary is taxed completely differently from the same withdrawal after you stop working.
  • The gap between retiring and your state pension starting is often the lowest-tax window you will ever have. Drawing taxable money then, rather than later, is one of the few genuinely free improvements available — and you can test it by changing the draw order on the accounts page.

Checking your schedule

Enter your bands, then look at the tax report. The effective rate column is total tax over income plus withdrawals. Compare a year or two against a real tax calculation for your country. If it is close, the schedule is right; if it is systematically off, you have most likely entered net-of-relief contributions somewhere, or mis-set a wrapper's taxable fraction.

{% call note('warn', 'One tax unit, not two') %}

The model taxes the household as a single unit with one set of bands. Where a jurisdiction taxes individuals separately, this overstates tax for couples with uneven incomes — sometimes materially. It is the model's largest known simplification; see limitations.
