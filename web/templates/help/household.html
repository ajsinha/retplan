Changing "Retires at" stops contributions and starts the drawdown phase. It does not end a salary row — that is controlled by the row's own "To age" on the income page, because part-time work after retirement is common. When you move a retirement age, move the matching salary row too.

Choosing a planning age

Planning to life expectancy means roughly half of the outcomes outlive the money. Life expectancy is a median, not a horizon. Conventional practice is to plan to somewhere around the 10th-to-25th percentile of survival — commonly 95 for a single person and 97–100 for the second death of a couple.

The model does not draw a random age of death; it funds the plan to the age you set. To see how exposed you are, raise the age by five years and compare: a sharp drop in success means longevity is your binding constraint, and guaranteed income is the usual remedy.

The spending smile

Real spending in retirement is not flat. It typically declines through the sixties and seventies as travel and activity fall off, then ticks up late if care is needed. The smile is a multiplier curve by age, interpolated linearly between the ages you set, and clamped at both ends.

It applies only to spending rows flagged Age curve, so you choose which categories obey it. Housing and insurance usually should not; travel and hobbies usually should.

{% call note('tip', 'A conservative smile is a flat one') %}

If you would rather not assume spending falls, set every multiplier to 1.00. The smile then does nothing, and your plan is funded at full spending throughout. That is the cautious choice, and it costs you a slightly lower sustainable spend.

{% endcall %}

Survivor assumptions

When a person passes their planning age, their salary and pension rows stop except for the share set by Survivor % on each income row. Spending does not automatically fall — if you want it to, reduce the relevant rows or set their end ages. Two cannot live as cheaply as one, but nor does spending halve; 65–75% of joint spending is a common assumption.
