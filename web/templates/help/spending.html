Getting the total right

The most reliable method is top-down, not bottom-up: take twelve months of bank and card outflows, subtract transfers to savings and investments, subtract the mortgage (it is entered on the debt page), and the remainder is your spending. Then split that total into categories. Bottom-up budgets almost always come out low, because the irregular things get forgotten.

{% call note('warn', 'Do not enter the mortgage here') %}

Debt service comes from the debt page and is added automatically. Entering it as a spending row too will double-count it, and the projection will look far worse than reality.

The essential / discretionary split

This split is the model's flexibility. A guardrail policy cuts only the discretionary layer, so the split decides how much room the plan has in a bad decade. Be honest in both directions: calling everything essential removes your ability to adapt, and calling too much discretionary produces a plan that only works if you accept cuts you would not really accept.

A workable test for each row: if markets fell 30% and stayed down for five years, would I actually cut this? If yes, it is discretionary.

Lumpy costs

Cars, roofs, weddings and big trips are not annual, and averaging them into an annual figure hides sequence risk — a new car the year after a crash is a different event from one in a good year. Use Every N years with the real amount instead.

Healthcare and long-term care

Healthcare usually needs its own row with 1–3% above CPI, because medical costs have outpaced general inflation almost everywhere for decades.

Long-term care is not yet a dedicated module. Model it as an expense row: the annual cost, from the age care might start, for the expected duration, with a probability reflecting how likely it is, and 2% above CPI. The sample plan carries exactly such a row, switched off — switch it on to see what it does, because for many plans it is the single largest risk.

{% call note('tip', 'The one-row sanity check') %}

After entering everything, look at first-year spending on the dashboard. If it does not match what you actually spend today, the detail is wrong somewhere, and no amount of simulation will fix it.
