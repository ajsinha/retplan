A projection runs your current holdings forward thousands of times, each time with a different random sequence of returns, and reports the spread of where they end up. It is separate from the retirement plan's simulation: it works holding by holding, from real price data, over a horizon you choose (ten years by default), with your own contributions and withdrawals.

Running one

  • Years ahead (1–60) and whether the table is by year or by quarter. The simulation itself always runs quarterly; the choice only changes how the results are reported.
  • Return model — see the three models. Lognormal is the sensible default.
  • Trials. 5,000 is plenty for the median and the P10; use more if you care about P5 or a goal probability to the nearest point.
  • Goal (optional), in today's money: the value you hope to reach.
  • Money in and out: any number of contributions and withdrawals.
  • Under Assumptions, fees, rebalancing and stress: where expected returns come from, inflation, an annual fee, rebalancing, a percentage withdrawal, a crisis to open every trial with, the random seed, a name for the run, and your own return or volatility for any holding.
  • Run projection. The settings are remembered for this portfolio, and the run is saved: the last ten runs per portfolio are kept, each re-drawn exactly as it was from the stored numbers.

    Only priced holdings take part; an unpriced one counts as zero. A symbol held in two accounts is one asset. The weights are today's values, and they are the target mix that contributions buy and rebalancing restores.

    Where the assumptions come from

    Each holding needs three things: an expected return, a volatility, and its correlation with every other holding. The table Holdings as the model sees them shows all three and, in the From columns, exactly where each came from.

    Volatility: recent and long-run, blended

    Two estimates are available for most securities: the last year's realised volatility from daily returns (needs at least 60 trading days), and the long-run volatility from up to 20 years of monthly returns. They are blended with equal weight in variance:

    σ = √( ½·σ1y² + ½·σlong run² )

    so one calm year does not make the next thirty look safe, and one violent year does not make them look terrifying. With only one estimate, that one is used; with neither, the asset class's assumption. Cash always uses the cash assumption (1%).

    Expected return: mostly the class assumption

    The expected return is never the last year's average return. It starts from the asset class's long-run assumption (see the class table) and, depending on Expected returns from:

    {{ field_table([ ("blend (default)", "The class assumption, moved towards the security’s own long-run history in proportion to how much history there is.", "Weight on history w = min(years, 20) / 40, so 10 years of history gets 25%, 20 or more gets the maximum 50%. μ = (1 − w)·μ_class + w·μ_history."), ("history", "The security’s own long-run return alone.", "Tempting for a fund that has done well; see the warning below."), ("assumption", "The class assumption alone.", "Every equity fund then expects the same return, which is honest."), ("your override", "A figure you type for one holding.", "Wins over everything. Also available for volatility."), ]) }}

    History is used only when there are at least three years of it; otherwise the class assumption applies whatever the setting. The long-run return is converted from the monthly log returns (mean m, standard deviation s) to an arithmetic annual return: σ = s·√12, μ = exp(12m + σ²/2) − 1.

    {% call note('why', 'Why a year of prices cannot tell you the expected return') %}

    The uncertainty in an average return shrinks only with the square root of the time observed: the standard error of the mean is roughly σ / √T, with T in years. For an equity fund with 17% volatility and one year of data that is 17% / √1 = 17 percentage points — a fund that returned 25% last year has a true expected return anywhere from about −8% to +58% at 95% confidence. Even twenty years of data leaves 17% / √20 ≈ 3.8 points of uncertainty, which is why the history gets at most half the weight. Volatility is different: it is estimated from the spread of many daily returns, and a year of those pins it down well.

    {% endcall %}

    Correlation: the data, shrunk towards sense

    For each pair of holdings with at least 60 overlapping daily returns, the sample correlation is blended 70/30 with a long-run correlation for their two classes:

    ρ = 0.7·ρsample + 0.3·ρclass

    Pairs without enough overlap use the class figure alone: 0.9 within a class, 0 with cash, 0.5 with anything unclassified, and published-style long-run figures between classes (US and international equity 0.8, equity and bonds 0.1, and so on). A matrix assembled pair by pair can be internally impossible; if so it is shrunk towards no correlation, 1% at a time, by the smallest amount that makes it valid, and the run's notes say how much.

    {% call note('trap', 'Last year’s winner, projected forever') %}

    Choosing history, or overriding a fund with its recent return, is the most common way to build an absurd projection. A security's own past is a small sample of one path. Leave the default unless you have a reason you could defend to a sceptic.

    {% endcall %}

    The three return models

    Each quarter, every holding's value is multiplied by a random gross return. The models differ only in how that random number is drawn.

    Lognormal (correlated GBM)

    From each holding's arithmetic annual μ and σ, the lognormal parameters that reproduce them exactly are:

    s² = ln(1 + (σ / (1 + μ))²)     m = ln(1 + μ) − s²/2

    and a quarter's gross return is exp(m/4 + (s/2)·z), where z is a standard normal made correlated across holdings with the Cholesky factor of the correlation matrix. A value can never go below zero, and the median grows more slowly than the mean — at about μ − σ²/2 — which is real, not a flaw.

    Fat tails (Student-t, 5 degrees of freedom)

    The same, but each quarter the correlated normals are divided by one shared random factor, z·√((ν−2)/W) with W drawn from a chi-square with ν = 5 degrees of freedom. The scaling keeps the variance unchanged, so the average spread is the same; what changes is that extreme quarters are much more common. Because W is shared, a bad draw hits every holding at once — which is how crashes behave, and why diversification disappoints in them.

    Bootstrap of last year's daily returns

    Instead of a formula, the shocks are real history. Every overlapping 21-trading-day window of the stored year is taken, on the dates all risky holdings share, so each window carries the actual co-movement of your holdings on those days. A quarter is three windows drawn at random and added up.

    Each holding's windows are first centred on zero and rescaled so a quarter has exactly the blended volatility above; the drift m/4 is then added. The data supplies the shape — fat tails, skew, which holdings fall together — and the assumptions supply the level. A year holds only about twelve independent months, far too few to set either the mean or the volatility, which is why the level does not come from them. The bootstrap needs at least 84 common trading days; with less (one recently listed holding is enough) the run falls back to lognormal and says so.

    Money in and out

    {{ field_table([ ("Add / Withdraw", "A contribution or a withdrawal, per year.", "Add as many rows as you need; a row with no amount is ignored."), ("From year / To year", "Which projected years it runs in, inclusive.", "Year 1 is the next twelve months. From 1 to 10 is the whole of a ten-year run."), ("Amount / year", "The yearly amount in today’s money.", "Spread evenly: a quarter of it at the start of each quarter."), ("Inflation-linked", "Grows with the inflation assumption.", "Ticked (the default) keeps the amount constant in today’s money; unticked keeps it constant in currency units, so it shrinks in real terms."), ("Extra growth", "Growth per year on top of that.", "3% for contributions that rise with pay. Counted from the row’s first year."), ]) }}

    So a quarter's amount is amount/4 × (1 + growth)year − from × (1 + inflation)quarters elapsed / 4 (the last factor only when inflation-linked). Contributions buy the target mix. Withdrawals are taken pro rata from every holding.

    • Withdraw a % each year takes that percentage of the current value, a quarter of it each quarter, on top of any fixed withdrawals. It can never run the portfolio out; it just shrinks with it.
    • Running out. If a quarter's withdrawals exceed the value, what there is is taken, the value is zero, and that trial is counted as having run out from then on. Later contributions still arrive.
    • Annual fee: an adviser or platform charge on top of what the funds charge (fund costs are already inside their prices), taken quarterly as (1 + fee)¼ − 1 after returns.
    • Rebalance: back to the target mix at the end of every quarter, every fourth quarter, or never — in which case winners grow into a bigger share and the risk drifts.
    • Inflation is a fixed rate. Today's money is simply the future value divided by (1 + inflation)years.

    Reading the fan chart

    The x-axis is time; each point on it is the end of a year (or quarter). At each point, all the trials are sorted by value:

    • The inner band is P25 to P75: half of all futures end the period inside it.
    • The outer band is P10 to P90: eight futures in ten.
    • The line is the median, P50: half above, half below.
    • The expected-return path, when shown, is what happens if every holding earned exactly its expected return every quarter, with the same flows and fee. It compounds the arithmetic mean, so it normally runs above the median. The gap is volatility drag: the typical outcome of a volatile portfolio is below the outcome of its average return.
    • A goal line appears in today's money if you set one.

    Hover over a point for its percentiles, the median return that period, and the share of futures that have reached the goal or run out by then.

    {% call note('trap', 'The median line is not a path') %}

    No single future looks like the median line. The P50 at year 5 and the P50 at year 10 are usually different trials, and every real trial is far more jagged. That is what Three futures, year by year is for: the actual trials that ended nearest the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of real final value, period by period. Notice that even the middle one has losing years.

    The CSV

    The CSV button downloads the period table in both bases, one row per year or quarter, with these columns: period, years, nominal_p5 … nominal_p95, real_p5 … real_p95 (P5, P10, P25, P50, P75, P90, P95), mean_nominal, return_p10, return_p50, return_p90, return_mean, median_cagr_to_date, contributions, withdrawals_median, p_loss_in_period, p_depleted_by_end, p_above_start_real (share whose real value is at least today's value) and, with a goal, p_goal_by_end. Returns and probabilities are fractions (0.05 is 5%).

    Seeds and reproducibility

    The same holdings, prices and settings with the same seed give the same answer, to the last digit. But prices change every day, so re-running tomorrow will differ slightly even with the same seed. To see whether a setting matters, change only that setting and keep the seed; to see how much of a result is noise, change only the seed.
