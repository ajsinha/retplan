{{ goto('wizard', 'Open the quick-start wizard') }}

The full editor asks for everything a careful plan needs: wrappers, tax bands, regimes, glide paths, draw order. The wizard asks for about twenty numbers instead and fills in the rest from the sample household, so you get a first answer in a few minutes. Nothing it assumes is hidden — every row it creates is an ordinary row you can open and change in the editor afterwards.

The six steps

{% call steps() %}
  • You. A name for the plan, your age, the age you retire and the age to plan to. Tick I'm planning with a partner for a second person with their own three ages.
  • Income. Pay before tax, real pay growth, and a public or state pension with the age it starts. Other income (rent, part-time work, an annuity) sits under a fold.
  • Savings. Your accounts, taken from a portfolio's accounts in RetPlan, or chosen by type by hand - 401(k), Roth 401(k), IRA, Roth IRA, HSA, brokerage, savings, pension pot, tax-free savings - with what goes into each every year (and, by hand, each one's balance).
  • Spending. What you spend now, the share you expect in retirement, the essential share, what you want to leave, and an optional mortgage (not asked when the accounts come from a portfolio: its debts are used).
  • Assumptions. An investment mix, a tax choice and inflation.
  • Review. The plan in words. Each group has a Change link back to its step; then choose how to save it and press Build my plan.
    • {% endcall %}

    Your answers are kept as a draft in your browser session as you go, so Back and a page reload lose nothing. Start over clears the draft. Each step is checked when you press Continue, and any problem is described in words on the same step.

    What is checked

    • Your age (and your partner's) is between 16 and 100.
    • The retirement age is not before the current age — if you have already retired, enter your current age.
    • The plan-to age is after both the current age and the retirement age.
    • No balance is negative, spending is above zero, and the essential share is between 0 and 100%.
    • An investment mix is chosen, and inflation is between −2% and 15%.

    Every step is re-checked when you press Build my plan, so a draft that was edited out of order still cannot produce an impossible plan.

    Savings from a portfolio

    If you track your accounts in RetPlan and have gathered them into a portfolio, the Savings step offers to take the portfolio's accounts: pick the portfolio and press Use it, or arrive from a portfolio's Start a plan from it. The step then lists the portfolio's investment and cash accounts and asks only what goes into each every year: workplace plans — 401(k), Roth 401(k), pension pot — as a % of pay plus the employer's match, the others as an amount a year. 529 plans are skipped. The Spending step no longer asks about a mortgage, because the portfolio's debts come across as loans. The plan it builds is linked to the portfolio: balances, holdings, property and debts are brought up to date every time the plan runs. See using a portfolio in your retirement plan.

    Savings by hand: one account per type

    Without a portfolio, tick each kind of account you have. Each one is taxed differently, and that decides how much of a withdrawal you keep, when you must start withdrawing, and which account to spend first - so a dollar in a Roth IRA lasts longer than a dollar in a 401(k). Each ticked type becomes an account (one each for you and your partner where accounts are personal) on a tax wrapper with that type's rules:

    Limits are for 2026 and are held in today's money. RetPlan keeps each account's saving within its own limit; it does not add a 401(k) and a Roth 401(k) together against the one shared limit, so keep their combined rate within it. Workplace plans are paid a share of their owner's pay, with the employer's match on top; the others a fixed amount a year. Ages in the rules are the account owner's. Brokerage and cash accounts start with no gains in them; if yours has large unrealised gains, lower its cost basis in the accounts editor. The draw-order tool tests whether another order pays less tax.

    Saving works through the household's surplus — income left after spending, debt and tax. Your % of pay goes to the retirement account first, then the fixed amount to taxable investments, and whatever is still left over is swept into taxable investments too. So the saving figures decide mainly where money goes first; unspent income is never lost. Contributions stop when the household has retired.

    {% call note('trap', 'The employer figure is a match') %}

    "Employer matches up to" is a match: 100% of your own percentage-of-pay saving, up to that share of pay. If you enter 0% for yourself, the employer adds nothing. The match is new money - it does not come out of your surplus - but it follows what you actually managed to save: in a year with too little surplus to make your full contribution, the match shrinks with it.

    Spending: four rows

    Your annual spending is split by the essential share, and split again at retirement:

    • Essentials (working years) and Discretionary (working years): today's full level, from now until you retire. Skipped if you are already retired.
    • Essentials (retirement) and Discretionary (retirement): the retirement share of today's level (never below 10%), from retirement for life. Only the retirement discretionary row follows the age spending curve (the "smile"); essentials stay flat in real terms.

    All four are in today's money. Rows that come out at zero are left out. The essential/discretionary split matters later: a flexible withdrawal policy can cut discretionary spending in a bad year but never the essentials. A mortgage becomes one amortising loan at the rate and remaining term you give. Leave at least becomes the plan's legacy target.

    Investment mix and the glide path

    {{ field_table([ ("Conservative", "35% equity, 35% government bonds, 20% corporate credit, 10% cash.", "Smaller swings, lower growth."), ("Balanced", "60% equity, 25% government bonds, 10% corporate credit, 5% cash.", "The default."), ("Growth", "85% equity, 10% government bonds, 5% corporate credit.", "Bigger swings, more growth."), ]) }}

    The mix is applied to the retirement, tax-free and taxable accounts. Each of them then glides in a straight line to the conservative mix, starting ten years before your retirement age (or now, if that is sooner) and finishing five years after it. Choosing conservative therefore means no glide at all. The cash account is 100% cash and never glides. The sample's property and alternatives classes start at zero.

    Saving the result

    • Save as a new scenario (the default) creates a new plan named after the wizard's plan name and makes it active. Your current plan is left as it was, and you can compare the two on the {{ goto('compare', 'comparison page') }}.
    • Replace the current plan overwrites the active plan — its inputs and its name — with the wizard's plan. There is no undo; download the current plan as JSON first if you might want it back.

    Either way you land on the dashboard with the deterministic projection already drawn. Press Run simulation for the odds.
